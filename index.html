Can you actually buy what the model wants to hold?

A token research desk where every number has to prove itself

Twenty tokens profiled for tradable liquidity, value accrual to holders, and how much a portfolio can own before the market stops absorbing it.

17of 20

positions hit their liquidity cap in a $1B portfolio — at that size it becomes a different strategy.

Situation
Crypto screens rank tokens by daily volume. A fund pays the order book, and needs to know whether a protocol’s fees ever reach the people holding its token.
What I did
Walked live Coinbase and Kraken books for $100K–$10M orders, split protocol fees from holder revenue, capped each position at 10% of median daily volume, and put every memo number behind a verifier.
Outcome
AAVE trades $256M a day but has $1.6M within 2% of price on US venues. On a point-in-time universe since 2016, a $100M fund has fit every month since August 2020; a $1B fund never has for long.
Open the live desk Code and methodology
My role
Research questions · liquidity and capacity design · verification rules · reviewing every memo conclusion against governance records
Method
Order-book walks for $100K–$10M orders · Amihud illiquidity · fees vs. token-holder revenue · Ledoit-Wolf covariance · positions capped at 10% of median daily volume · point-in-time universe since 2016, dead coins included

Reported volume and tradable depth tell different stories. The desk measures both, writes a memo per token from a registry of facts, and refuses to publish if any number in a memo does not match its source. A language model may add commentary; a deterministic verifier decides whether it ships.

The verifier guarantees a number matches its source, not that the conclusion is right. When a memo said Aave’s holder cash flow had stopped, I checked governance before believing it — the DAO had paused buybacks.

Can an LLM be your marketing analyst?

Two AI analysts, two opposite mistakes

One stopped at the naive number. The other controlled away the campaign’s own effect. Getting it right meant knowing which variables the campaign itself caused.

−0.02pp What a formal-looking regression reported
it controlled for impressions and clicks, which the campaign causes
+5.5pp With pre-treatment controls only
OLS, matching and weighting all agree
Situation
A targeted, non-randomized campaign across 5,000 users showed a +5.53 point lift in conversion. I gave two language models the same data and asked whether the campaign caused it.
What I did
Ran the analysis with pre-treatment controls only — OLS, propensity matching and inverse-propensity weighting — and audited each model’s answer against it.
Outcome
The campaign works: every correctly specified estimate lands between +5.5 and +6.0 points. Gemini reported the naive number as causal; the regression Claude Code wrote controlled for the mediators and collapsed it to zero.

The campaign was targeted, not randomized, so the naive lift mixes the campaign with who was chosen for it. The trap is the next step. Impressions and clicks are caused by the campaign; controlling for them measures its effect with its own mechanism held fixed, which is why a regression with four controls reports zero. With baseline covariates only, OLS, matching and weighting agree, and the treated and control groups overlap almost completely on their pre-treatment propensity.

Rigor is specification discipline, not variable count. A regression that controls for a mediator is more dangerous than a naive comparison, because it looks like method.

My role
Study design · the specification · the audit each model was judged against
Method
Naive difference · OLS with pre-treatment covariates · 1:1 propensity matching · inverse-propensity weighting · mediation decomposition

Asked directly, Gemini rated its own confidence in the causal claim 4 out of 10 — and in the plain prompt still reported the naive 5.53 as the effect.

Who actually gets hurt on Massachusetts roads?

Massachusetts crash intelligence

Raw counts say the problem is motor vehicles, because that is where the volume is. Severity rates say something else entirely.

11.1×

A pedestrian crash is 11.1 times as likely as a motor-vehicle crash to kill or seriously injure someone.

Situation
Massachusetts publishes every crash, but raw counts point at motor vehicles, because that is where the volume is.
What I did

Outcome
Ranked by severity instead of volume, the picture reverses: pedestrians and cyclists carry the risk. The bicycle killed-or-serious share fell 27.6% even as volume rose.

When is the obvious answer wrong?

The aggregate said nothing. The sub-categories did not.

A difference-in-differences around the September 2025 H-1B proclamation that reads as a clean null — until you stop trusting the average.

My role
Data pipeline · grouping rules · the disaggregation that found it
Method
65,360 POI-month rows filtered to 37,524 on a privacy-reliability threshold · postal codes grouped into tech, university and control areas · mix comparison to check the control group was comparable

At the aggregate level there is nothing to report — the contrasts are small and point in inconsistent directions. Disaggregated, a pattern appears in exactly the places where daily life happens, and it appears on the largest samples in the dataset.

The finding only appeared after I stopped trusting the average — and it survives because it sits on the biggest samples, not the biggest percentages.

Charcoal, ballet, and the things that are not on a résumé

Between Memory and Invention

Six charcoal drawings · 2025

I made this series in March 2025 during my third year at Brandeis University in Waltham, Massachusetts. Living through a New England winter, far from the landscapes I grew up with, I found memories of the Aegean and Mediterranean returning through the act of drawing.

I began without predetermined compositions, at times working on three drawings simultaneously and allowing them to develop alongside one another. I had long been drawn to perfection and planning; here, I wanted to release that impulse. Charcoal became central to that process. I worked it directly with my hands, diffusing, disturbing and erasing it, then returned with charcoal pencils to create sharper marks. Its ability to move between atmosphere and precision allowed both control and surrender.

Although I approached the works as abstraction, forms emerged as I worked: oceanic rhythms, vegetation, landscapes, and fragments of places and memories. Having spent much of my childhood around the Aegean Sea, its patterns remain part of my visual memory. In Massachusetts, those impressions surfaced indirectly rather than as literal depictions.

The drawings move between recognition and dissolution. A mark can suggest a landscape or remembered place and then return to abstraction. The resulting works exist somewhere between memory and invention, between what is remembered and what is being made in the present.

Resolution

Drag to pull back

Pull the slider back and the same sheet you just looked at goes from texture to drawing. Nothing on the paper changed — only the resolution you read it at.

That is not a metaphor I reached for afterwards. It is the same failure I spend my working hours guarding against: the H-1B study on the other page returns a clean null at the aggregate and a real pattern one level down, on the same rows. Reading at the wrong resolution is how you miss a finding — and, just as often, how you invent one that was never there.
