Investment Management May – Aug 2026

AI-augmented multi-asset meta-labeling

Graduate Field Project · Brandeis × State Street, Boston

A research pipeline that sits on top of a multi-asset signal book and decides which signals to act on. A primary layer produces directional views; a meta-labeling layer learns when that primary view is trustworthy, so the strategy sizes down instead of trading everything it sees.

My work covered the attribution design — index-level rather than ETF-level returns, technical signals consolidated into one sleeve, transaction costs applied in two layers, and benchmark-relative active weights — plus walk-forward validation across folds, so nothing is read off a single lucky sample.

Running my own P&L for three years taught me to distrust a backtest that has never paid a transaction cost. That is why the cost model here has two layers.

Marketing Analytics Spring 2026

Can an LLM be your marketing analyst?

Causal inference versus language-model reasoning on the same A/B test

I gave a language model the same marketing campaign data I analysed properly, and checked whether it could tell correlation from causation. The dataset: 5,000 users, ten campaigns, a binary ad-exposure treatment, pre-treatment covariates and conversion outcomes.

The naive treatment-minus-control difference is a 5.53 point lift and it is highly significant. It is also entirely selection bias: users who saw more impressions were already the more engaged users. Control for the covariates and the effect goes to zero. Propensity score matching does not rescue it either — the two groups separate almost perfectly on propensity, so there is no overlap to match on. That failure is itself a result: not every method works on every dataset.

Given the same summary statistics, the model cited unconfoundedness, SUTVA and the need for regression controls — and then accepted the 5.53 figure anyway. It knew every concept and could not execute one of them. Its confidence also moved with the framing of the question, which is the part that should worry anyone putting a model in an analyst’s chair.

The most dangerous way to use one of these models is to ask it to confirm something you already believe. Given a leading prompt it pushed back — and then engaged with the biased framing anyway.

The aggregate said nothing. The sub-categories did not.

Difference-in-differences on Boston–Cambridge foot traffic · Python, Tableau

A natural experiment around the September 2025 H-1B proclamation, using Advan point-of-interest visit data for Boston and Cambridge in January 2025 and January 2026. I built the pipeline: 65,360 POI-month rows, filtered to 37,524 after dropping counts below the privacy-reliability threshold, then grouped by postal code into tech, university and control areas, with a mix comparison to check the control group was actually comparable.

At the aggregate level there is nothing to report — the difference-in-differences contrasts are small and point in inconsistent directions. Disaggregated, a pattern appears in exactly the places where daily life happens, and it appears on the largest samples in the dataset.

I built the pipeline and the grouping rules. The finding only appeared after I stopped trusting the average — and it survives because it sits on the biggest samples, not the biggest percentages.

Who actually gets hurt on Massachusetts roads

Crash analysis dashboard · Python, Streamlit, SQLite, MassDOT IMPACT API

A dashboard over the state's open crash record system — 661,000 records across 2021–2025, pulled live from MassDOT's ArcGIS feature services, normalised into SQLite, and filtered by date, road user, hour and street. Built on an MVC split so the data layer, the filters and the views stay independently testable.

The finding that reframed the project: raw crash counts say the problem is motor vehicles, because that is where the volume is. Severity rates say something else entirely. I also assembled a small panel of municipal traffic-board approvals — stop signs, stop-ahead treatments, reflective striping — with their approval dates, so specific intersections can be studied before and after an intervention.

Telco churn — where the accuracy number lies

Logistic regression, random forest and XGBoost on 7,000 subscriber records. Ovrule — decision receipts for AI agents

Creator & sole developer · Next.js, Supabase, LLM classifier, npm SDK

Every other guardrail reads what an agent says. Ovrule reads what the action does — who authorised it, whether it can be undone, how much breaks if it is wrong. A refused action never runs, and every verdict leaves a signed receipt written in a sentence a compliance team can actually read.

Built end to end: schema, classifier, evaluation set, API, interface, and an npm SDK that wraps an existing tool call in two lines. Selected for Demo Day at Ship Season S26.

For three years every decision in my company was mine to defend. I know exactly what it costs when nobody can answer “who approved this?”

Resolution

At one magnification there is nothing here but texture. At another there is a drawing. Nothing on the paper changed — only the resolution you looked at it with.

Between Memory and Invention

Six charcoal drawings · 2025

I made this series in March 2025 during my third year at Brandeis University in Waltham, Massachusetts. Living through a New England winter, far from the landscapes I grew up with, I found memories of the Aegean and Mediterranean returning through the act of drawing.

I began without predetermined compositions, at times working on three drawings simultaneously and allowing them to develop alongside one another. I had long been drawn to perfection and planning; here, I wanted to release that impulse. Charcoal became central to that process. I worked it directly with my hands, diffusing, disturbing and erasing it, then returned with charcoal pencils to create sharper marks. Its ability to move between atmosphere and precision allowed both control and surrender.

Although I approached the works as abstraction, forms emerged as I worked: oceanic rhythms, vegetation, landscapes, and fragments of places and memories. Having spent much of my childhood around the Aegean Sea, its patterns remain part of my visual memory. In Massachusetts, those impressions surfaced indirectly rather than as literal depictions.

The drawings move between recognition and dissolution. A mark can suggest a landscape or remembered place and then return to abstraction. The resulting works exist somewhere between memory and invention, between what is remembered and what is being made in the present.
