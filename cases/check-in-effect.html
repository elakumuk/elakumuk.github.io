Behavioral Finance Lab · Study 01

Independent research
Experimental design

Proposed protocol

Historical stress episode
2 × 2 randomized design

Research question

Does seeing investment losses more often change what people feel—and what they do?

A proposed experiment protocol separating feedback frequency from allocation flexibility.

The Check-In Effect is Study 01 of the Behavioral Finance Lab. It uses the 2020 COVID-19 drawdown and recovery as a historical market stimulus. This is a proposed protocol and interface prototype—not a completed human-subject study, financial forecast, or investment recommendation. No participants or results.

2 × 2

The proposed design varies how often participants see performance and how often they may change an allocation, separating feedback frequency from decision opportunity.

Contribution vs. prior work
This is a conceptual replication and extension of Bellemare et al. (2005), who experimentally separated feedback frequency from choice flexibility. The proposed extension replaces lottery outcomes with a fixed historical market-return path, uses a continuous 0–100% risky allocation, targets adults with recent equity-investing experience, and adds repeated state-response measures. The causal separation itself is not claimed as novel.
Historical episode
January–December 2020 U.S. equity-market monthly returns, including the COVID shock and subsequent recovery. The archived stimulus is the 202608-vintage monthly Fama/French factor series from Kenneth French’s Data Library. For risky weight w, monthly portfolio return is RF + w × (Mkt-RF); the remaining weight earns the one-month Treasury bill rate. All arms receive the same underlying returns; this is an experimental stimulus, not a causal analysis of real 2020 investors.
Primary outcome
Terminal risky-asset allocation at the common 12-month endpoint. Everyone is observed at the same horizon, including participants who had fewer opportunities to revise their allocation. The pre-period starting allocation is elicited in every arm and may be used as a prespecified baseline covariate. Common quarter-end allocations and repeated state ratings are secondary outcomes.
Psychological outcomes
Perceived-risk and unease ratings at shared quarter-end checkpoints, after the allocation decision, so every condition receives the same measurement prompts at the same points. Because ratings follow the choice, post-choice justification may influence them; this is a design tradeoff, not a clean measure of pre-decision emotion. Pre-task investment experience and self-reported risk attitude are separate baseline context, not one blended “investor psychology score.”
Factor A · feedback
Frequent (monthly) versus infrequent (quarterly) performance reports.
Factor B · decision opportunity
Frequent (monthly) versus infrequent (quarterly) ability to revise allocation. Proposed 2 × 2 between-subject random assignment; exact timing and comprehension checks to be finalized in pilot.
Primary estimand
Intention-to-treat marginal effect of frequent versus infrequent feedback on terminal allocation, averaged across randomized decision-frequency arms. Estimate the 2 × 2 factorial model; report the feedback-by-decision interaction and confidence intervals as a prespecified secondary contrast.
Data integrity
The frozen 12-month source file is archived with provenance and a SHA-256 hash. The monthly portfolio formula is documented; the prototype applies a constant target allocation with monthly rebalancing. No empirical study estimates or results are presented.
Power & sample
Do not invent a sample size. Define the smallest effect of practical interest, estimate plausible parameters from prior work and a feasibility pilot, and use simulation-based power analysis for the primary marginal feedback contrast. Assess interaction power separately rather than implying it is guaranteed by the main-effect calculation; account for the single-path stimulus and its potentially weak treatment contrast.
Analysis safeguards
Pre-specify the primary outcome, exclusions, missing-data handling, manipulation checks, and exploratory outcomes. Report effect sizes and confidence intervals; account for repeated choices and participant heterogeneity. Label moderator analyses exploratory unless powered and preregistered.
Hypotheses
H1: More frequent feedback lowers terminal risky allocation (directional, based on myopic-loss-aversion theory). H2: Feedback frequency changes reported state risk/unease (secondary). H3: Feedback and decision frequency may interact (secondary; power separately). Any mediation claim is exploratory because a measured emotional response is not itself randomized.
Psychological measurement
Separate baseline risk-taking propensity from state response. Candidate baseline: the published DOSPERT Financial Investment domain, which distinguishes risk-taking likelihood from perceived risk and expected benefits. Candidate state measures: a direct perceived-risk item and valence/arousal ratings informed by SAM, after the allocation at shared quarter-end checkpoints. The demo’s “uneasy” slider is provisional, not a validated scale; conduct cognitive interviews and pilot the final wording before preregistration.
Ethics & limits
No real money, brokerage data, or financial advice. The public demo stores no responses. Any recruitment or identifiable data collection requires review of applicable Brandeis IRB requirements before launch. A retrospective scenario experiment cannot establish how investors actually behaved during 2020.

Prototype

The browser-only demo uses the archived historical market series to illustrate the proposed decision flow; its client-side random assignment is not balanced research randomization. It collects no data and is not evidence about participants. The study still needs comprehension testing, finalized measures, simulation-based power analysis, preregistration, and ethics review before recruitment.
